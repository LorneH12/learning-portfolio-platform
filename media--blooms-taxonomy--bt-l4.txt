Allow 4 minutes. Alignment means the objective names the intended performance, practice prepares learners for it, and assessment elicits evidence of it. Carnegie Mellon’s alignment guidance illustrates why recall-only tests do not establish an analytical objective.

Worked example: objective “Judge an escalation proposal against impact, staffing, and response-time criteria.” Practice: compare two sample proposals, see a worked critique, then judge a third using those criteria. Assessment: select the strongest judgment of a new proposal and its reason. This selected-response assessment samples judgment; an independently written critique would provide additional evidence of the learner’s reasoning.

Misaligned version: the same objective, followed only by a slide defining escalation and a quiz asking for the definition. The problem is not that definitions are useless. They may be necessary preparation. The problem is that the promised performance is never practiced or assessed.

If the objective is Create, choosing somebody else’s plan is insufficient evidence of original production. Ask the learner to produce an artifact and assess it against transparent criteria. Do not claim that the multiple-choice assessment in this sample verifies creation skill; its objectives deliberately focus on classification and selection.
